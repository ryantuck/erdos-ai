## Summary

The page (OPEN, prize \$500) asks: for a countably infinite $A\subset(1,\infty)$ with $|kx-y|\ge1$ for all
$x\ne y\in A$ and integers $k\ge1$, is $A$ sparse? In particular, is $\sum_{x\in A}\frac1{x\log x}<\infty$, or
$\sum_{x<n,\,x\in A}\frac1x=o(\log n)$? For integers the condition makes $A$ primitive, and Erdős [Er35]
proved the convergence of $\sum\frac1{n\log n}$ for primitive sets. Koukoulopoulos, Lamzouri and Lichtman
[KLL25] proved the $o(\log n)$ form for the general case, and the first sum remains open.

The Lean has one definition (`ErdosSeparated`) and two theorems, one for each sum.

## A. Semantic fidelity

**A1. Statement fidelity: pass.**

- **Source** (verbatim): "Let $A\subset (1,\infty)$ be a countably infinite set such that for all $x\neq y\in A$
  and integers $k\geq 1$ we have $\lvert kx -y\rvert \geq 1.$ Does this imply that $A$ is sparse? In particular,
  does this imply that $\sum_{x\in A}\frac{1}{x\log x}<\infty$ or $\sum_{x <n,\ x\in A}\frac{1}{x}=o(\log n)$?"
- **Back-translation of the input.**
  - `erdos_problem_143a`: "For every injective sequence $a:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R$ with $a_i>1$ and
    $|ka_i-a_j|\ge1$ for all $i\ne j$ and $k\ge1$, the series $\sum_i\frac1{a_i\log a_i}$ converges."
  - `erdos_problem_143b`: "For the same sequences, $\frac1{\log n}\sum_{i:\,a_i<n}\frac1{a_i}\to0$."
- **Checks.**
  - *Sets and sequences.* A countably infinite $A\subset(1,\infty)$ is the range of an injective sequence, and
    every injective sequence has a countably infinite range. The terms of both sums are positive, so
    summability and the value of the sum do not depend on the enumeration. The condition is imposed on
    distinct indices, which are distinct elements because $a$ is injective.
  - *The hypothesis `ha_inj` is redundant.* With $k=1$ the separation says $|a_i-a_j|\ge1$ for $i\ne j$, so
    $a_i=a_j$ forces $i=j$. `variants.separated_injective` proves this in Lean. It is harmless, and
    `erdos_problem_143b` needs injectivity so that the sum counts each element once.
  - *Countability* is implied by separation (a $1$-separated subset of $\mathbb R$ is countable), so the word
    on the page adds nothing, and so the sequence form loses nothing.
  - *The hypotheses are satisfiable.* `variants.primes_separated` proves in Lean that the primes, in
    increasing order, satisfy `ErdosSeparated`: for distinct primes $p,q$ the integer $kp-q$ is non-zero,
    because $kp=q$ would give $p\mid q$. So neither theorem is vacuous.
  - *The sums.* `1 / (a i * Real.log (a i))` is positive since $a_i>1$, so `Summable` is convergence of the
    series of positive terms, the page's $\sum_{x\in A}\frac1{x\log x}<\infty$. In 143b the sum
    $\sum_{x<n,\,x\in A}\frac1x$ is `∑' i, if a i < n then 1 / a i else 0`, with the page's strict inequality.
  - *The `tsum` is a finite sum.* `variants.finite_below` proves in Lean that $\{i\mid a_i<x\}$ is finite for
    every $x$: distinct terms differ by at least $1$, so their integer parts are distinct and lie in
    $\{0,\dots,\lceil x\rceil\}$. So `tsum` is the honest sum over $x\in A$, $x<n$, and no junk value arises.
  - *"o(log n)".* `Tendsto (· / Real.log n) atTop (𝓝 0)` is $o(\log n)$. At $n=0,1$ the logarithm is $0$ and the
    quotient is $0$ in Lean, but `atTop` ignores finitely many $n$.
  - *"Or".* The page offers two alternative formulations of sparseness, and the prize of [Er97c] is for
    "resolving the questions in the main problem statement". The input has one theorem for each, which loses
    nothing. The page does not define "sparse", and the two sums are its "in particular".
  - *The relation between the two.* `variants.b_of_a` proves in Lean that 143a implies 143b: if
    $\sum\frac1{x\log x}$ converges then $\sum_{x<n}\frac1x=o(\log n)$. So 143b is the weaker form, which
    agrees with its being the one that [KLL25] could prove. It also confirms that the two input statements are
    consistent with each other. The proof splits the sum at an index $m$ with small tail and uses
    $\frac1x=\log x\cdot\frac1{x\log x}\le\log n\cdot\frac1{x\log x}$ for $x<n$. It needs no separation.
