## Summary

The page (OPEN, \$100) asks whether every set of $n\ge5$ points in the plane has two distances that
occur for at least one and at most $n$ pairs of points, and whether the number of such distances
tends to infinity. It records that the largest distance is always one (Hopf–Pannwitz), that $n=4$
fails, that $n=5,6$ hold (Erdős–Fishburn), that convex position holds (Clemen–Dumitrescu–Liu), and
that $n^{1-o(1)}$ such distances may be true.

The Lean has the right shape, but it counts ordered pairs against $n$, which halves the threshold.
The first theorem is then false. The reference keys are undefined.

## A. Semantic fidelity

**A1. Statement fidelity: defect (fixed).**

- **Source** (verbatim): "Let $A\subset \mathbb{R}^2$ be a set of $n$ points. Must there be two
  distances which occur at least once but between at most $n$ pairs of points? Must the number of such
  distances $\to \infty$ as $n\to \infty$?"
- **Back-translation of the input's first theorem:** "Every set of $n\ge5$ points has at least two
  distances, each occurring for between $1$ and $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ unordered pairs."
- **[defect: ordered-pair-threshold]**
  - `pairCount A d` is the number of ordered pairs $(x,y)$ with $x\ne y$ and $\mathrm{dist}=d$, so it
    equals twice the number of unordered pairs.
  - `IsLimitedOccurrence A d` requires `0 < pairCount A d ∧ pairCount A d ≤ A.card`. So it allows at
    most $n/2$ unordered pairs, not $n$.
  - The page's "pairs of points" are unordered, and the Hopf–Pannwitz bound of $n$ is tight for the
    regular $n$-gon with $n$ odd, which has $n$ unordered pairs at its largest distance.
- **The first theorem is false as formalized** (machine-checked). Take $(0,0),(2,0),(0,2),(2,2),(1,1)$.
  The squared distances are $4$ (side), $8$ (diagonal) and $2$ (centre to corner). The unordered counts
  are $4,2,4$, so the ordered counts are $8,4,8$. Against the bound $n=5$ only the diagonal is limited,
  so there is exactly one limited distance, not two. `variants.first_pass_part1_false` proves this for
  the first pass's definition.
- **Numerical check on further configurations.** "Page" counts unordered pairs against $n$, and "input"
  counts ordered pairs against $n$.

  | configuration | $n$ | unordered multiplicities | limited, page | limited, input |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | regular pentagon | 5 | 5, 5 | 2 | **0** |
  | square and centre | 5 | 4, 4, 2 | 3 | **1** |
  | regular hexagon | 6 | 6, 6, 3 | 3 | **1** |
  | regular heptagon | 7 | 7, 7, 7 | 3 | **0** |
  | $3\times3$ grid | 9 | 12, 8, 8, 6, 2 | 4 | **1** |
  | rhombus of two equilateral triangles | 4 | 5, 1 | 1 | 1 |
  | equilateral triangle | 3 | 3 | 1 | 0 |
  | five collinear points | 5 | 4, 3, 2, 1 | 4 | 2 |

  Under the page's reading the first five sets have at least two limited distances, as Erdős
  believed and Erdős–Fishburn proved for $n=5,6$. Under the input's reading all five fail.
- **The second theorem** (the number of limited distances tends to infinity) is affected too, because
  the notion of "limited" is stricter. The prior review called the effect "potential". Its truth under
  the input's definition is not known, and it is not the page's question.
- **v2:** the bound becomes `2 * A.card`, that is $n$ unordered pairs. `pairCount`, `limitedOccurrences`,
  and both theorems keep their text.

**A2. Lean semantics traps: pass, with the counting point in A1.**

- **`Finset (EuclideanSpace ℝ (Fin 2))`** is a set of $n$ distinct points, so `A.card = n`.
- **`0 < pairCount A d`** forces $d>0$, because distinct points are at positive distance. A distance
  that is not realized has count $0$ and is excluded.
- **`limitedOccurrences A`** is finite, since $d$ must be one of at most $\binom n2$ distances. So
  `Set.ncard` is exact and there is no junk value.
- **`open Classical`** supplies the decidability that `Finset.filter` needs on a real-valued predicate.
- **Quantifiers.** Part 2 is `∀ k, ∃ N, ∀ A, N ≤ A.card → k ≤ ncard`, with `N` depending on `k` only.
  That says the least number of limited distances over all sets of $n$ points tends to infinity.
- **The restriction $n\ge5$.** The page's first question has no restriction, but the answer is no for
  $n\le4$: for $n=4$ by the rhombus of two equilateral triangles, and for $n\le3$ because an
  equilateral triangle, a segment or a point has at most one such distance. The first theorem takes
  $n\ge5$, as Erdős believed.
