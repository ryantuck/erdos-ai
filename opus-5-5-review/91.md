## Summary

The page asks for a proof that, for every sufficiently large $n$, the $n$-point subsets of
$\mathbb R^2$ with the fewest distinct distances include at least two non-similar sets
("and probably many"). The status is OPEN.

The Lean theorem says something that looks stronger: there is $N$ such that for every
$n\ge N$, every $n$-point minimiser $A$ has an $n$-point minimiser $A'$ with
`¬ AreSimilar A A'`. The two are equivalent, by the argument in A1, because minimisers
always exist and `AreSimilar` is an equivalence relation. The definitions are correct.
The only shortfalls are scholarship (no references, no status) and missing small-case
remarks, which are added in v2.

## A. Semantic fidelity

**A1. Statement fidelity: pass.**

- **Source** (verbatim, including the page's typo): "Let $n$ be a sufficently large
  integer. Suppose $A\subset\mathbb R^2$ has $\lvert A\rvert=n$ and minimises the number of
  distinct distances between points in $A$. Prove that there are at least two (and probably
  many) such $A$ which are non-similar."
- **Back-translation of `erdos_problem_91`:** "There is $N$ such that for all $n\ge N$ and
  every finite $A\subset\mathbb R^2$ with $|A|=n$ whose distinct-distance count is $\le$
  that of every $n$-point $B$, there is an $n$-point $A'$ whose count is also $\le$ every
  $n$-point $B$'s and which is not similar to $A$."
- **Equivalence with "at least two non-similar minimisers exist" (for each $n\ge N$):**
  - *Minimisers exist.* $\{\#\mathrm{dist}(B) : |B|=n\}$ is a nonempty set of naturals,
    since collinear $n$-sets exist, so its minimum is attained. Hence Lean ⇒ source: take
    any minimiser $A$, and Lean supplies a non-similar $A'$.
  - *`AreSimilar` is an equivalence relation.* A map with
    $\mathrm{dist}(f x,f y)=r\,\mathrm{dist}(x,y)$, $r>0$, is $r$ times an isometry of
    $\mathbb R^2$. Such an isometry is affine and injective, hence bijective. Conditions (3)
    and (4) then give $f(A)=B$. So $f^{-1}$ (ratio $1/r$) gives symmetry, composition gives
    transitivity, and the identity gives reflexivity.
  - Hence source ⇒ Lean. Let $B_1\not\sim B_2$ be minimisers and $A$ any minimiser. If
    $A\sim B_1$ then $A\not\sim B_2$, else $B_1\sim B_2$; so take $A'=B_2$. Otherwise take
    $A'=B_1$.
- **"Sufficiently large"** is `∃ N, ∀ n ≥ N`, the standard reading.
- **"Non-similar"** is `¬ AreSimilar`. `AreSimilar` allows reflections, which is the
  standard notion of similarity.
- **Not vacuous.** The hypothesis is satisfiable for every $n$, as shown above. The
  conclusion cannot be met by a degenerate $A'$: scaled, rotated or reflected copies of $A$
  are similar to it.

**A2. Lean semantics traps: pass.**

**B2. Variants and completeness: pass for the problem proper; enrichment added.**

- The problem box has a single assertion, which is formalized.
- The remarks contain checkable small cases:
  - $n=3$: the equilateral triangle is the unique minimiser.
  - $n=4$: the square and the rhombus of two equilateral triangles are two non-similar
    minimisers.
  - $n=5$: the regular pentagon is the unique minimiser [Ko24c].
  - $6\le n\le9$: Erdős says there are two non-similar minimisers.
  - "(and probably many)".
- All five are added in v2, using one packaging definition, `IsDistinctDistanceMinimiser`,
  built only from the file's own constructs.
- **Verification of the $6\le n\le9$ claim** (review scratch script, floating point with
  tolerance $10^{-9}$):
  - Distance counts: regular $k$-gons have 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 4 distances for
    $k=3,\dots,9$. The regular $(k-1)$-gon with its centre has 2, 3, 3, 3, 4, 5 distances
    for $k=4,\dots,9$.
  - In the 19-point hexagonal patch of the triangular lattice (radius 2), 36 nine-point
    subsets determine at most 4 distances. One example, writing $a+b\omega$ with
    $\omega=e^{i\pi/3}$, is
    $\{(a,b)\}=\{(-2,0),(-2,1),(-2,2),(-1,-1),(-1,0),(-1,1),(0,-1),(0,0),(0,1)\}$. Its squared
    distances are exactly $\{1,3,4,7\}$, so it has 4 distances.
  - Minimality uses classical classifications. The largest planar 2-distance set has 5
    points, the regular pentagon (attributed to Kelly). The largest planar 3-distance set
    has 7 points (attributed to Shinohara). Both attributions are recalled from memory and
    are not verified against a source in this container, so they appear in this review only,
    not in the Lean file.
  - So the minima are 3, 3, 4, 4 for $n=6,7,8,9$. They are attained by two non-similar sets
    each: one in convex position, one with an interior point. Erdős's claim holds.
- The "probably many" variant is a natural reading of the parenthetical, not a
  page-stated theorem. It is labelled OPEN and stronger than the main statement.

   accent)". Both captures read "Kovács", and the `/latex/91` extraction gives "Z. Kovács".
3. **Hallucinated title.** It attributes to the website "[Er87b] … *Some combinatorial,
   probabilistic and number theoretic problems* (1987)". The `/latex/91`, `/latex/654` and
   `/latex/1069` extractions all give *Some combinatorial and metric problems in geometry*,
   Intuitive geometry (Siófok, 1985), 167–177.

   - The conclusion, that "every minimiser has a non-similar minimiser" is equivalent to

   bare proposition, which is what the raw input under review uses.
7. **Correct observations.**
   - The asymmetry of `AreSimilar` is only superficial.
   - The whole-plane requirement in `AreSimilar` is harmless. Its wording "restricts to
