## Summary

The problem (Tarski's circle-squaring problem, from the Scottish Book era, posed on the
site from [Er81b, p.30]): "Can a square and a circle of the same area be decomposed into
a finite number of congruent parts?" — solved affirmatively by Laczkovich [La90b], who
proved it is possible using translations only. The Lean theorem
(`conjectures/1124.lean:26`) asserts: there exist $n$ and two $n$-indexed families of
subsets of $\mathbb{R}^2$ partitioning the closed unit square and the closed disk of
radius $1/\sqrt{\pi}$ (both of area 1) respectively, with each square-piece carried onto
the corresponding disk-piece by a distance-preserving map. Read back independently, this
is exactly the affirmative answer to the question as asked, with a WLOG-sound choice of
representatives. I found **no semantic defects**; the findings are scholarship gaps (no
bibliography in the file, missing [Er81b] source attribution) and a page-confirmed
enrichment (the translations-only strengthening), all applied in
`conjectures-v2/1124.lean`.

## A. Semantic fidelity

**A1. Statement fidelity — pass (source-verified).**

- Recovered verbatim source statement (see Addendum): "Can a square and a circle of the
  same area be decomposed into a finite number of congruent parts?"
- Independent back-translation of lines 26–38: "there exist $n$ and families
  $(S_i)_{i<n}$, $(D_i)_{i<n}$ of subsets of $\mathbb{R}^2$ such that the $S_i$ are
  pairwise disjoint with union $[0,1]^2$, the $D_i$ are pairwise disjoint with union the
  closed ball $\bar B(0, 1/\sqrt{\pi})$, and for each $i$ some distance-preserving
  $f_i : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ has $f_i(S_i) = D_i$." This is the standard
  formal meaning of "the square and the disk of equal area are equidecomposable with
  congruent pieces" — the question's content, asserted affirmatively.
- Representative choice: the source quantifies over "a square and a circle of the same
  area"; the Lean fixes the unit square and the disk of radius $1/\sqrt{\pi}$ (areas
  both 1: $\pi (1/\sqrt{\pi})^2 = 1$). Any equal-area square/circle pair is the image of
  this pair under a single similarity applied to both sets simultaneously, and
  equidecomposability by isometries is invariant under that, so the instance is
  equivalent to the general statement — faithful, not a restriction.
- "Circle" = closed disk: Tarski's problem is about the disk (the site tags it geometry
  and the solution reference is Laczkovich's circle-squaring paper, whose theorem covers
  the closed disk). Closed square vs closed disk is the standard reading.
- Indexing: both families share the index type `Fin n`, which correctly encodes the
  bijection between pieces ("corresponding parts"). No 1-vs-0-indexing issue arises
  (nothing depends on index order).
- Not vacuous / not trivially false: $n = 0$ is impossible (empty union ≠ nonempty
  square); $n = 1$ is impossible (a rigid motion would make the square congruent to the
  disk, contradicting e.g. that one boundary contains segments and the other does not);
  the statement's truth is exactly Laczkovich's deep theorem. Empty pieces are permitted
  but harmless in both directions: empty pieces can be freely added or dropped without
  affecting any conjunct (an isometry image of $\varnothing$ is $\varnothing$).

**A2. Lean semantics traps — pass.**

- No ℕ subtraction anywhere.
- Division: the only division is `1 / Real.sqrt Real.pi` (line 33); $\sqrt{\pi} > 0$, so
  no division-by-zero convention is engaged.
- No `Finset.range`, no bounded quantifiers over ℕ, no filters/limits — none of the
  off-by-one or vacuity traps arise. The only universal quantifiers are over `Fin n`
  (piece indices) and `Fin 2` (coordinates, in `unitSquare`), both used correctly.
- `Disjoint (pieces_sq i) (pieces_sq j)` on sets is `pieces_sq i ∩ pieces_sq j ⊆ ∅` —
  correct pairwise disjointness; together with the union condition this is exactly a
  partition into (possibly empty) parts.
- **`Isometry f` semantics checked:** Mathlib's `Isometry` requires distance
  preservation but *not* surjectivity. This is still exactly congruence: a
  distance-preserving self-map of $\mathbb{R}^n$ preserves metric betweenness (equality
  in the triangle inequality), hence maps lines to lines and is an affine rigid motion,
  automatically surjective. Independently, any isometry between two *subsets* of
  $\mathbb{R}^2$ extends to a rigid motion of the plane, so `∃ f, Isometry f ∧ f '' S =
  D` holds iff $S$ and $D$ are congruent in the classical sense. No defect.
